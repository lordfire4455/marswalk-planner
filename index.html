        How sure are we?
        • Checked against NASATerrain heights. The CTX elevation model agrees with Perseverance's own localization at all 708 rover waypoints: mean difference 0.9 m, spread 3.4 m.
        • Checked against NASAMars clock. Reproduces the sol numbers and sunrise times in NASA's Perseverance and Curiosity weather feeds.
        • Checked against NASARoute choice. On 40 stretches of Perseverance's real drive (40.7 km), 79% of the planner's route lies within 100 m of the path JPL's rover drivers chose. Turn on "Planner vs Perseverance" in Layers to see it.
        • Checked against NASAScience targets. Each sample site is the rover's surveyed position on the sol the sample was taken.
        • ModelTime, energy and oxygen. Apollo-derived walking and metabolic models (Marquez 2008; Santee et al. 2001), as in NASA / MIT SEXTANT. Planning estimates, never measured on Mars.
        • ModelWeather. A seasonal outlook from 4,745 sols of Curiosity data and Perseverance reports, not a forecast.
        • RelativeMinerals at stops. CRISM band strengths (weak / moderate / strong) from the 2007 orbital scenes, about 18 m per pixel. They show where a mineral is likely, not how much of it there is.
        • Not visibleRocks and ripples under 20 m. The grid cannot see them. Check the last metres in the HiRISE layer and log what the crew finds as field reports.

        How the planner picks a route
        1. The ground is cut into 20 m squares from NASA's CTX elevation model of Jezero, and every square gets a slope.
        2. Squares steeper than your slope limit are closed. The route's grid steps never land on one.
        3. Every step is scored by the time it takes to walk it, using the Apollo walking model: slow uphill (a 5° climb cuts speed by more than half) and slow on steep downhill.
        4. That time is multiplied by a slope penalty: ×1.6 on a 10° slope, ×2.4 at 15°, ×3.5 at 20° and ×4.9 at 25°.
        5. An A* search finds the path with the lowest total score, moving in 16 directions from each square. So the route often walks farther to stay on gentle ground. Usually that is only a few percent, but in steep country it can add kilometres; the comparison below shows how much on each leg.
        6. If a stop sits on ground that is too steep, the route ends at the nearest safe square within 8 squares in each direction (up to about 220 m).

        Below, each leg is compared with the straight line between its two points and with the quickest path your limit allows (the same search without the slope penalty).

          Real-world uses beyond this map

          The engine needs only an elevation model, so the same safety logic carries over to:

          • Artemis Moon walksslope, suit time, oxygen and sunlight at the lunar south pole
          • Rover drive planningfirst-pass routes, already shown to match Perseverance's
          • Field geology and analog missions on Earthsafe traverses to sampling sites
          • Search and rescue in mountainsfastest legal route under a slope limit
          Built to the challenge briefWhat the app does for each requirement, and where to see it
          Requirement 1A single place, not a global map

          One 30 × 30 km site, Jezero Crater, on a 20 m elevation grid with 25 cm imagery. Every walk starts at a base and runs through numbered waypoints.

          Requirement 2Five kinds of NASA data, layered

          Optical, topographic, spectral, thermal and atmospheric data from six missions. Each layer switches on and off and names its instrument.

          Requirement 3Crew safety and suit limits

          Slope limits for a suited astronaut, safest-path routing, walking time, energy and oxygen, daylight, cold, dust and radio cover, in one GO / NO-GO verdict.

          Requirement 4Science along the route

          21 targets at places Perseverance and Ingenuity actually worked, each with what to investigate and why. The planner suggests targets near your path.
